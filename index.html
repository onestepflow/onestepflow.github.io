When One Step Is Enough:
An Empirical Study of Euler Integration
in Flow-Matching Policies

Shivakanth Sujit*, Luca Nunziante*, Rousslan Dossa, Kai Arulkumaran

Araya Inc., Tokyo, Japan

* Equal contribution

Accepted at the 10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026)

November 9–12, 2026 · Austin, Texas, USA

Paper BibTeX

Project Overview

For large, sufficiently trained vision-language-action models, a single Euler step largely matches multi-step task success, with 1.6× to 4.8× faster action generation across the evaluated VLAs. No retraining, distillation, or auxiliary objectives are required.

Abstract

Flow matching is now the dominant approach for vision-language action (VLA) robot policies, thanks to its expressiveness and simple training objective. However, inference for standard flow matching requires iteratively solving an ODE, limiting the control frequency at deployment. While several works have proposed novel methods to allow for single-shot action generation, we ask whether such additional objectives are actually necessary. We find that across six flow matching VLAs, one-step action generation largely matches multi-step sampling at scale: multi-step integration confers little to no consistent advantage at inference. We empirically find that this holds as long as models are sufficiently trained and have enough capacity. Practically, the implication is that for large VLAs, the single-step policy comes essentially for free from the multi-step model, enabling low-latency deployment without retraining, distillation, or auxiliary objectives.

Experimental Results

We compare six publicly released VLAs, spanning 450M to 4.7B parameters, and a 211M DiT baseline across four simulation benchmarks. For each model and benchmark, we use the same checkpoint and matched seeds, changing only the number of Euler integration steps.

Key Findings

  • Step-equivalence depends on scale, VLM pretraining, and training maturity. π₀.₅ and XR-0 are equivalent within 3 pp on every simulation benchmark with an available checkpoint, and X-VLA and LAP on all but one each. Pretrained visual-language features favor step-equivalence even without robot-action pretraining, and the gap fades with longer fine-tuning.
  • Longer task horizons expose step sensitivity. The two smaller VLAs (FLOWER, SmolVLA) keep a gap that is larger on long-horizon tasks, while their short-horizon performance is robust. Extra Euler steps do not always help: the direction of the gap varies across the smaller models.
  • No systematic real-world advantage for multi-step inference. Pooled gaps on the Franka suite are small and run in both directions, and one-step motion is no less smooth for any model above 1B parameters.
  • Task success and behavioral diversity can differ. On D3IL Avoiding, one-step inference preserves success while narrowing the range of successful strategies.
  • Practical payoff. A single Euler step gives 1.6× to 4.8× lower action-generation latency for the evaluated VLAs.

Real-World Evaluation

We evaluate four kitchen-style tasks with a Franka Emika Panda arm using paired, blinded A/B testing. The suite includes 4,800 rollouts, with 100 trials per task for each model and inference variant. Models are fine-tuned on our in-house dataset, and the policy noise is seeded within each pair.

Multi-step inference confers no systematic advantage. Only 3 of 24 task–model comparisons show a significant McNemar difference, and they run in both directions: the reference is better on Close Oven for π₀.₅ and FLOWER, while one step is better for FLOWER on Open Drawer. Pooled per-model gaps are small and bidirectional, with 4 of 6 models within ±2 pp. LAP is the only model whose pooled CI excludes zero (McNemar p = 0.0545), so a genuine multi-step advantage cannot be ruled out there. The physical evaluation budget does not establish equivalence within ±3 pp.

Trajectory Smoothness

We compare the RMS joint jerk of paired real-world rollouts in which both variants succeed. There is no consistent smoothness cost to one-step inference: the multi-step reference is meaningfully smoother only for the two sub-1B models (DiT, FLOWER), while for every model above 1B, one-step motion is marginally smoother.

Trajectory Diversity

D3IL Avoiding is a multimodal control task with 24 valid ways to succeed. We built a visual version of the task and run 200 episodes per model with matched initial states and policy noise. One-step inference improves success for DiT and FLOWER, lowers it for X-VLA, and never hurts the larger models. It does, however, narrow behavior: under one step, DiT, FLOWER, and LAP each collapse onto a single successful strategy while keeping at least 99.5% success, and π₀.₅ drops from 19 observed successful strategies to 8.

Ablations

We study how initialization and training length shape the step-count gap on LIBERO, which pairs the short-horizon Spatial, Goal, and Object (SGO) suites with the long-horizon LIBERO-10 suite, where most gaps appear.

Model scale and pretraining. A randomly initialized π₀.₅ favors one-step inference throughout training and has not reached step-equivalence by the final checkpoint, although its SGO gap narrows from −20 to −3 pp. For both π₀.₅ and X-VLA, starting from a generic VLM (PaliGemma2 or Florence), from the VLA's own VLM backbone, or from the full pretrained VLA all reach near-zero SGO gaps, so robot-action pretraining is not required. Pretrained features alone are not enough, though: the 211M DiT, built on pretrained CLIP encoders, develops a multi-step advantage on LIBERO-10 that grows to 15 pp.

Training maturity. π₀.₅ is step-equivalent on SGO from the earliest checkpoint. For X-VLA, extra Euler steps hurt early in training, and that gap collapses by the end. FLOWER's multi-step benefit is confined to LIBERO-10 at early and intermediate checkpoints and disappears at the final one. Its residual gap in Table 2 therefore reflects the authors' early-stopped release checkpoint, not the model's scale or architecture.

Action divergence vs. task success. For the π₀.₅ initialization variants, the L2 distance between 1-step and 10-step actions and the success-rate gap shrink together over training. But the pretrained variants can show large action divergence with a gap under 5 pp, while the from-scratch model turns a smaller divergence into an 18 pp gap. How far the two actions disagree does not by itself determine task success.

Inference Latency

A single Euler step reduces per-chunk inference latency for the six VLAs by 1.6× to 4.8× on an NVIDIA A100. Speedups depend on the relative cost of the VLM backbone and action head.
